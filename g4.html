Overview/Summary/Goal 4 — worker-quality drivers

Goal 4 — Trust-weighted curriculum findings

The deep-dive behind the Questions, Courses and Reviewers theme pages. Each step shows the punchline, the method, the data, and the caveats.

What we measured

Five steps + four extensions, all centered on the worker's PDR (Performance Defect Rate) over the last 3 trusted reviews. PDR < 0.05 = good; PDR > 0.95 = bad.

What we found

After filtering untrustworthy reviewers (Step 1), per-question predictivity (Step 2/3) separates the curriculum into a green and a red list. Mistake clustering (Step 4/5) and user-trait analysis (Step 6/6.5) point at curriculum and hiring fixes.

Step 1 — Filter untrustworthy reviewers

Dropped 4 of 40 audited reviewers → 883 trusted reviews remain across the project (273 good [QM≥4], 333 bad [QM≤2], 277 mid [QM=3]). Note (2026-06-10): the GOOD cutoff is now QM≥4 (was ≥3) to align with the project's promotion gate ("2 consecutive 4+ at L−1 full history"). QM=3 (accept_with_fixes) is treated as MID rather than GOOD.
Definition

For each reviewer in our Goal-2 audit sample (currently 41 audits, 9 reviewers), compute a trust score = (n_defensible + 0.5 × n_questionable) / n_audited. Apply the majority_unjustified drop rule: any reviewer where more than 50% of their audits came back feedback_unjustified is removed from the pool.

The 893 surviving reviews are tagged as good (quality_overall_qms ≥ 3) or bad (quality_overall_qms ≤ 2). These two pools feed steps 2-3 (good) and 4-5 (bad).

Caveats / what could be wrong
  • Small audit sample. Only 41 of 200 sampled reviews are audited so far (and 159 more not yet sampled would push to 359). 6 of 9 reviewers have N ≤ 5 audits each — a single new audit can flip the trust score by 20pp.
  • N=1 edge case. bobrow.taylor has only 1 audit (which came back unjustified). Strict reading of majority_unjustified drops them, but the evidence is weak. Re-evaluate after more audits.

  • Unaudited reviewers are kept by default. Roughly 30 reviewers across the full project have not been audited yet. Their reviews are still counted in steps 2-5 with no trust signal applied. Set --audited-only on step-1 to exclude them.

Step 2 — Course questions PREDICTIVE of better project performance

Of 55 (course, question) pairs with enough workers (n ≥ 10): 10 are predictive (r_pdr < −0.05 — passing the question correlates with a LOWER defect rate downstream).
Definition

Predictor: per worker, did they get this course question correct? (0/1, from Q-G1.questionScoresBreakdown[qid].score; best attempt per worker if multi-attempt.)

Outcome (PRIMARY): per worker, PDR = Performance Defect Rate = fraction of the worker's last 3 trusted reviews where quality_overall_qms < 4 (below the promotion gate). Range [0, 1]. Lower = better. Rolling window is configurable via --rolling-window-n; set to 0 to use all reviews.

Secondary outcome: trusted mean_qm over the same window (continuous, 1–5). Kept in the CSV as r_meanqm for inspection but no longer the primary signal.

Stat: point-biserial r between predictor (binary) and outcome (PDR). Range [−1, +1]. NEGATIVE r means passers have a LOWER defect rate (the GOOD direction).

Flag: a question is "predictive" iff r_pdr < −0.05. The full ranking is in analysis/g4_question_predictive.csv.

Derivation

Code: analysis/run_g4.py step-2

  1. Read trusted reviews. For each worker, join via Q-C.WORKER_ID = attemper of the reviewed_attempt.
  2. Per worker compute PDR over the last 3 trusted reviews (and mean_qm + pct_good as side metrics).
  3. Read Q-G1. For each (worker, course, question) extract pass/fail (score ∈ {0,1}).
  4. For each (course, question), compute point-biserial correlation between pass-state and PDR. Require n ≥ 10 workers.
  5. Also compute mean PDR for passers, mean PDR for failers, and Δ (Δ negative → passers do better).

Caveats
  • Effect sizes are small. Top |r| is roughly 0.13–0.20 — conventionally a "small" effect (Cohen). Useful as a screening signal in aggregate, not a deterministic single-question filter.
  • PDR is bounded. With a rolling window of 3, PDR can only take values {0, 0.33, 0.67, 1.0} — fewer distinct values than mean_qm. This deflates correlation magnitudes slightly vs the old mean_qm-based numbers.
  • Fisher z CI is added by run_g4_qdiagnose stats. Re-run that to refresh r_pdr_ci95_lo/hi after this metric switch.
  • 52 of 55 questions lack a body because they live in a nested sub-section structure our Q-G2 walker didn't reach. Those rows show "(question text not in Q-G2 SECTIONS export)" — the correlation is computed correctly, we just can't display the wording.
  • The outcome is the reviewer-graded defect rate, not promotion or task-completion rate. Predictive of "fewer reviewers reject the work", not "task survives end-to-end".

Step 2.5 — Combinations: does passing N of the predictive questions matter?

For each course, we built a composite score = how many of the predictive questions a worker passed, and grouped workers by that count. Bars are mean PDR with 95% CI; dashed line at PDR=0.50 marks where half the worker's recent reviews fall below the promotion gate. LOWER bars = better.
Definition

For each course, take the questions where r_pdr < −0.05 from step 2 (the "predictive" set: passing the question is associated with a LOWER defect rate downstream). For each worker who took the course, compute a composite score = number of those predictive questions they passed. Group workers by composite score; report mean PDR ± 95% CI per bucket. Also report Pearson r between composite score and PDR (NEGATIVE r = more passes → fewer defects). For context, raw all-question pass-count is shown too.

Caveats
  • "Predictive" is defined by single-q r_pdr < −0.05 from step 2. That set may itself be noisy at small effect sizes — the composite inherits any noise.
  • 95% CI on the bucket means uses a normal approximation (z=1.96). For small buckets (n < 30) Welch t intervals would be more conservative; the CI shown is therefore slightly tight at low n.
  • Composite Pearson r treats "n_passed" as continuous, but it's really an integer count with a small range. Spearman rank-order would be a more conservative test; results would be similar.
  • This is descriptive correlation. We're NOT claiming course passage causes lower defect rate — the same trait may drive both.
  • PDR ceiling. Because PDR ∈ {0, 0.33, 0.67, 1.0} for the rolling-3 window, the bar chart granularity is coarse. The dashboard's Step 2.7 plots the same data using mean_qm_in_window for a continuous view.

For each course, we searched every subset (size 1–5) of its questions to find the bundle whose carriers (workers who passed ALL questions in the bundle) have the most extreme mean PDR. Definition

For each subset, compute mean PDR of workers who passed ALL questions in the subset vs workers who didn’t. Surface the best (most-negative Δ PDR) and worst (most-positive Δ PDR) subset per course with n_carriers ≥ 10.

Caveats
  • Small carriers for some subsets. Counterproductive bundles often have n right at the floor (10–20) — enough to surface a pattern but not enough for tight CIs. Treat the Δ magnitude as directional, not precise.
  • Co-occurrence inflation. Several courses have many subsets with identical Δ — that’s because adding "easy" questions to a subset doesn’t change who carries it (almost everyone passes them). The reported “best subset” is the smallest version of the equivalence class.
  • Synergy isn’t isolated here. We report Δ PDR, not synergy uplift. Some subsets just inherit the strength of one good single question. See g4_q_subset_all.csv’s synergy_uplift column for the multi-question-bonus metric.
  • This is descriptive correlation, not causal. Passing question X doesn’t MAKE a worker good; the trait that makes them good probably also helps them pass X.

Step 2.7 — Tag-based ground-truth check (cross-validating Step 2)

Step 2 ranks questions by their correlation with our derived PDR. This step cross-validates that ranking against a completely independent signal: the platform’s pre-existing openclaw_rl_trusted_reviewer (known-good) and openclaw_rl_bad_quality (known-bad) worker tags. A question that's truly predictive should ALSO have a higher pass-rate among the known-good cohort than the known-bad cohort.

4 questions agree on "predictive" (both signals point down on defects); 0 agree on "counterproductive"; 2 CONTRADICT each other and need a manual look.
Definitions

PDR per worker = fraction of their last 3 trusted reviews scored below the promotion gate (QM < 4). Lower PDR = better. Across 495 workers in the trusted cohort: mean PDR = 71.62%; 103 workers have PDR=0 (all recent reviews ≥4), 312 workers have PDR=1.0 (no recent reviews ≥4), 80 are in between.

Δ PDR = (mean PDR of question passers) − (mean PDR of question failers). Negative = passers have fewer defects.

Tag separation = (pass rate among workers carrying openclaw_rl_trusted_reviewer) − (pass rate among workers carrying openclaw_rl_bad_quality). Positive = the question correctly discriminates known-good from known-bad. Negative = the question is INVERSELY related to known quality (red flag).

Why two signals? Δ PDR uses OUR derived trust-weighted outcome (Steps 1 + 2). Tag separation uses the platform’s pre-existing known-good / known-bad tags as a SEPARATE ground truth. When both signals agree, the question's classification is double-confirmed. When they CONTRADICT, the question is suspicious and should be manually re-graded.

Derivation

Code: analysis/run_g4_pdr.py. Outputs: g4_worker_pdr.json, g4_question_pdr.csv.

Defect definition: any trusted review where QM < 4 (below the promotion gate). The threshold is configurable via --good-qm-min. Rolling window over last 3 reviews matches the cohort used in steps 2 onward.

Caveats
  • PDR = 1.0 dominates. Because mean_QM in this cohort hovers around 2.9, most workers have all 3 recent reviews below QM=4 and end up with PDR=1.0. That collapses information — there's no way to distinguish "always fail by a little" from "always fail badly". For a finer-grained metric, use mean_qm_in_window (also in g4_worker_pdr.json).
  • Tag separation requires both tag groups to be present in the question's join sample. If a question has only trusted-tagged carriers (no bad-tagged), tag_separation is null and the question is excluded from this section.
  • Why might the two signals disagree? (a) The platform's trusted_reviewer / bad_quality tags are set by different criteria than our trusted-mean-QM (they may include behaviours like rejection rate, not just QM); (b) Sample selection — tagged workers may have taken courses in a different period than the cohort in our PDR window; (c) The question is genuinely picking up a confounder.
  • Don’t act on a contradiction without reading the question. Use the diagnosis Step 7 (LLM per-question) to understand WHY before recommending a fix.

Step 4 — MISSING from courses (mistakes the curriculum doesn't teach)

14 of 22 mistake clusters are weakly covered (total_coverage_score ≤ 2). Combined, they account for 275 of 663 bad-review records (41.5%) — these are your curriculum gaps.
Definition

Mistake cluster: a recurring category of mistake mined by an LLM from the 341 bad-review (QM≤2, trusted reviewer) quality_feedback texts. 22 clusters identified.

Coverage score (0–3): for each (cluster, course) pair, a second LLM pass reads the course's section titles + question stems and rates how clearly the course addresses the mistake. 0 = not addressed; 3 = explicitly tested by a question.

Curriculum gap = weakly covered: total_coverage_score ≤ 2 across all courses combined. (0 means zero coverage anywhere; 1 means only one course tangentially mentions it; 2 means two courses tangentially mention it or one mentions it more substantively.)

Derivation

Code: analysis/run_g4_mistakes.py (step-4a / 4b / 4c)

  1. step-4a: Pull every bad-review's quality_feedback text from CDS payloads → g4_mistakes/bad_feedback.jsonl (341 records, ~80K tokens).
  2. step-4a: Pull per-course section titles + sample of question stems → g4_mistakes/course_summary.json.
  3. step-4b: LLM (claude opus, effort=max) reads bad_feedback.jsonl + grading_rubric.md, emits 22 mistake clusters with names + descriptions + example task_attempt_ids.
  4. step-4c: LLM reads clusters + course_summary, scores each (cluster, course) pair 0–3.
  5. Aggregate per cluster: total_coverage_score = sum of scores across all courses. Filter rows where it ≤ 2.

**Curriculum Gaps (no course scores above 1):** Nine clusters have effectively no meaningful coverage — only tangential or inferred mentions at best. The most impactful gaps by record volume are: C05 source_inspiration_invalid (35 records) has no dedicated section on approved source platforms or invalid source types; C06 silver_trajectory_not_iterated (31 records) has no section teaching the silver trajectory correction protocol; C09 prompt_unnatural_overstructured (25 records) has no instruction on natural, conversational prompt style; C11 single_turn_multi_turn_mismatch (20 records) has no section on turn-type constraints; C12 temporal_data_outside_universe_range (20 records) has no section on validating date ranges against universe boundaries; C16 memory_md_not_triggered_by_prompt (17 records) has no section on the MEMORY.md triggering requirement; C08 fields_internal_misalignment (26 records) has no section specifically on cross-field coherence between AO, CF, and DO. Collectively, these gap clusters account for ~280 of the 341 feedback records.

**Course-Fail Signals (score ≥ 2 but high error volume):** Three clusters have clear course coverage yet remain among the most frequent mistakes — signaling that the courses are either insufficient in depth or not being retained. C01 prompt_do_misalignment (128 records, score 2 in Common Errors Screening) is the single largest cluster and appears to be addressed in training, yet over a third of all feedback cites it. C03 entities_data_not_in_universe (62 records, score 2 in both Universes and Common Errors courses) persists despite a dedicated Universes course with good-vs-bad examples. C13 safety_annotations_incorrect_or_missing (19 records, score 2 in both "Almost Right" and Screening Course) is covered by two separate courses with dedicated safety sections, yet workers still misidentify or omit safety failures. These patterns suggest that course content exists but may lack sufficient practice exercises, worked examples of the specific failure mode, or post-course reinforcement to drive behavior change at task-authoring time.

Step 5 — Covered but NOT WORKING (course teaches it, workers still make the mistake)

Of the strongly-covered clusters (total_coverage_score ≥ 3), 18 (cluster, course) pairs still show workers who PASSED the relevant course making the mistake in production. The biggest example: prompt_DO_misalignment (128 records) IS taught in the Common Errors Screening (score 2) yet still accounts for over a third of all bad reviews.
Definition

For each (cluster, course) pair where the course covers the mistake (coverage_score ≥ 1), count workers who:

  1. Had a bad-review attempt that the LLM tagged as an example of this cluster, AND
  2. Had passed the course (Q-G1.STATUS = 'pass').

High count + high % = course content isn't transferring to behavior. Likely fix is more practice exercises, worked examples, or post-course reinforcement.

Derivation

  4. Count workers where STATUS = 'pass'.

Big caveat — example IDs are capped, percentages are NOT real rates

The LLM cluster output (step-4b) limited example_task_attempt_ids to ~5 per cluster (it was asked for "3 examples"). So n_workers_in_cluster is capped at ≤5 in nearly every row, and pct_failing_despite_pass clusters around 100% just because the denominator is tiny. To fix this properly: re-run step-4b asking the LLM to emit ALL matching attempt_ids per cluster (not just 3 examples). Then step-5 auto-recomputes. The pos-count curve (more good tags → lower PDR) and the net-score curve quantify how stacking good tags together moves the defect rate. Caveats
  • Tag co-occurrence: Many of these tags appear on overlapping populations. TCM_* tags are nearly always co-present — a worker with one TCM tag tends to have the others. So a "size 4" subset of all TCM tags isn't 4 independent signals; it's mostly the same population as size 1 with one of those tags.
  • Synergy uplift is the true new-information metric. A subset with high |Δ_PDR| but ≤0 synergy uplift is just being carried by its strongest individual tag. A subset with positive synergy is genuinely more predictive than any tag alone.
  • Small carriers: our cutoff is n≥10. The very top subsets cluster around n=10–20, so the CIs on Δ are wide. Don't over-interpret a single subset's Δ down to the third decimal.
  • PDR ceiling effect: because PDR is bounded [0, 1] and most workers have PDR=1.0 in this cohort, the magnitudes shrink at the extremes. Use the +Step 6.5 net-curve below as the cleanest ordinal view.
